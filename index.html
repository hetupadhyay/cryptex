                    The one that can’t be broken
                    
        
                    Machines made ciphers far harder to crack, and the most famous of them fell anyway. One cipher never has. It’s the honest exception to every secret having a weakness, and it comes with conditions.
                    
      
                    

      
                    
        
                    
          Germany, 1926 to 1945
          
                    Enigma
                    
          
                    Three rotors scramble each letter, a reflector sends it back through them, and a lamp lights the result. Type a message or press the keys.
                    
        
                    

        
                    
          
                    
            
                    
              
                    
                
                    
                  
                  
                    
                  
                  I
                
                    
                
                    
                  
                  
                    
                  
                  II
                
                    
                
                    
                  
                  
                    
                  
                  III
                
                    
              
                    
              
                    
            
                    

            
                    
              
              
            
                    
            
                    
              Lamps
              
            
                    
            
                    
              
              
            
                    
            
                    The rotor letters are the start position. Set them, then type.
                    
            
                    
              
              
              
                    Each pair swaps two letters on the way in and on the way out. Wartime operators plugged ten pairs.
                    
              
                    
            
                    
          
                    

          
                    
            
                    Why it’s harder than Caesar
                    
            
                    Caesar uses one alphabet for the whole message. Enigma’s right rotor turns before every letter and carries the others along like an odometer, so each key press uses a different alphabet, and the pattern only repeats after 16,900 letters. Press A five times and watch.
                    
            
                    Add the choice of rotors and the plugboard and there were about 159 million million million settings. At a billion a second, trying them all would take some 5,000 years.
                    

            
                    How Bletchley Park read it anyway
                    
            
                    Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski broke the early version in 1932 and handed their methods to Britain in 1939. Nobody tried every setting. The weaknesses were elsewhere.
                    
            
                      
              
                    • Cribs. Weather reports went out at the same times in the same words, so codebreakers could guess a stretch of plaintext, like WETTERVORHERSAGE, weather forecast.
                      • 
              
                    • A letter never encrypts to itself. The reflector makes sure of it. A guessed word can only sit where none of its letters lines up with the same letter, which rules out most positions at once.
                      • 
              
                    • Operator habits. Lazy start positions like AAA, the same greetings, the same sign-offs, day after day.
                      • 
              
                    • The bombe. Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman built on the Polish bomba: a machine that ran through rotor positions against a crib and threw out every one that led to a contradiction.
                      • 
            
                    
          
                    
        
                    
      
                    

      
                    
        
                    
          1882 and 1917
          
                    The one-time pad
                    
          
                    Add a random letter to every letter of the message, wrapping round after Z. A counts as 0, B as 1, and so on. That’s the whole cipher.
                    
        
                    

        
                    
          
                    
            
                    
              
              
            
                    
            
          
                    
          
                    Message + pad = ciphertext
                    
          
                    
          
                    The pad comes from your browser’s cryptographic random number generator. Nobody, this page included, could have predicted it.
                    
        
                    

        
                    
          
                    Now make it say something else
                    
          
                    Type any other message with the same number of letters. Here is the pad that turns the same ciphertext into it.
                    
          
                    
            
            
          
                    
          
                    Ciphertext − this pad = your message
                    
          
                    
          
                    
          
                    Both pads look just as random. Without the real one there’s no way to tell which message was sent, because every message of this length is equally possible. In 1949 the Bell Labs mathematician Shannon proved what that means: the ciphertext says nothing at all.
                    
        
                    

        
                    
          
                    Three rules, no exceptions
                    
          
                      
            
                    1. Truly random. From dice, noise or a hardware generator, never a phrase or a pattern someone chose. A pad taken from a book is just a long key full of English.
                      2. 
            
                    2. As long as the message. One pad letter for every message letter. A shorter key has to repeat, and a repeating key is Vigenère, which the codebreaker’s desk takes apart.
                      3. 
            
                    3. Used once, then destroyed. Use a pad twice and it stops being unbreakable.
                      4. 
          
                    
        
                    

        
                    
          
                    What a reused pad gives away
                    
          
                    Two messages, encrypted with the same pad. An eavesdropper sees only the two ciphertexts.
                    
          
                    
            
                    Ciphertext 1
                    
            
                    Ciphertext 2
                    
            
                    1 minus 2
                    
          
                    
          
          
                    A new pad changes both ciphertexts but never their difference. Subtracting one from the other cancels the pad and leaves message one minus message two, with no key at all. Guess that message one starts with MEET, take the difference away from it, and BRIN appears: the start of message two. American codebreakers read about 3,000 Soviet messages this way in the Venona project, after some pads had been printed twice.
                    
        
                    

        
                    So the headline has one honest exception, and only while every rule holds, every time. The pad has to be made, carried to the other side in secret and destroyed after use, and you need as much of it as everything you’ll ever send. That’s why it was kept for spies and for the hotline between Washington and Moscow, and why almost nothing else uses it.
                    
      
                    
    
                    

    
    
                    
      
                    
        
                    Why this matters today
                    
        
                    The padlock in your browser runs on the same ideas as everything on this page, rebuilt so the shortcuts are gone.
                    
      
                    

      
                    
        
                    
          Substitution and transposition
          
                    Confusion and diffusion
                    
          
                    AES, the cipher behind almost every https connection, works in rounds. Each round swaps every byte through a fixed table, then shifts and mixes the bytes across the block. Shannon, the same mathematician who proved the one-time pad unbreakable, named the two ideas confusion and diffusion in 1949. One round on its own would fall like any cipher here. AES-128 runs ten.
                    
        
                    
        
                    
          Key strength
          
                    Too many keys to try
                    
          
                    AES-128 has 2128 keys, about 3.4 × 1038. At a billion a second, trying them all would take around 1022 years, close to a trillion times the age of the universe. Every other cipher in the chart had a shortcut. In more than twenty years of public attacks, nobody has found one for AES that matters.
                    
          Compare it with the others
        
                    
        
                    
          Key distribution
          
                    Agreeing on a key in public
                    
          
                    The one-time pad sank on logistics: both sides needed the same secret before they could say anything. In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman showed how two strangers can agree on a shared key over an open line, while an eavesdropper reads every message. Your browser does this on every connection.
                    
          Back to the one-time pad
        
                    
      
                    

      
                    
        
                    
          
                    The first moment of an https visit
                    
          
                    All of this happens before the page appears, usually in a few hundredths of a second.
                    
        
                    
        
                      
          
                    1. 
            Browser → site
            
                      Hello
                      
            
                      Your browser lists the ciphers it supports and sends its half of a key exchange.
                      
          
                      2. 
          
                    2. 
            Site → browser
            
                      Proof
                      
            
                      The site sends its half, plus a certificate signed by an authority your browser already trusts, proving it really is the address in the bar.
                      
          
                      3. 
          
                    3. 
            Each side alone
            
                      Shared key
                      
            
                      Both sides combine the two halves into the same secret key. The key itself never crosses the network.
                      
          
                      4. 
          
                    4. 
            Browser ↔ site
            
                      Encrypted
                      
            
                      From then on every page, password and cookie is encrypted with AES or a cipher like it, and checked so nobody can change it on the way.
                      
          
                      5. 
        
                    
      
                    

      
                    
        
        
                    
          
                    Check it yourself
                    
          
                    On any https site, click the padlock, or the icon in its place, next to the address. Open the connection or certificate details and you’ll see who vouched for the site. Your browser’s developer tools go further and often name the key exchange and the cipher, such as X25519 and AES_128_GCM.
                    
        
                    
      
                    

      
                    The ciphers have stopped being the weak point. What breaks now is what broke Enigma: people. Reused passwords, convincing fake login pages, software nobody updated. Every secret still has a weakness. These days it’s usually us.
